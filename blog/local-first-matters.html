Why "Local-First" Matters — No Servers, No Tracking, No BS

Published July 27, 2026 · 5 min read

90% of Chrome extensions make zero dollars. That's not a market failure — that's a design failure.

When I started building browser extensions, I looked at the numbers: 138,000+ extensions in the Chrome Web Store, $42.5 billion market, 13-22% annual growth. And yet, 90% of extensions earn nothing. The top 1% — about 1,000 extensions — capture almost all the revenue.

The common advice is to find a niche, build something unique, market it well. But the more I studied the winners and losers, the more I noticed something else: the most profitable extensions aren't the ones with the best features. They're the ones with servers.

Servers mean recurring costs. Recurring costs justify subscription pricing. Subscription pricing generates predictable revenue. It's a neat little loop — if you can afford the server bill. The extensions that win are the ones that can justify a monthly charge, because that's what investors and acquirers want to see.

But there's another loop, and it's the one I chose to build on:

No servers → no recurring costs → no need for subscriptions → one-time pricing → no incentive to harvest user data → users trust you → they buy your next product.

What "local-first" actually means

It's become a buzzword. Every privacy tool claims to be local-first. So let me be specific about what it means for WayKnow products:

  1. All processing happens on your device. JSON parsing, screenshot capture, cookie management, color picking — every computation runs locally. No data is sent to a server for processing.
  2. Zero network requests from the core product. Open Chrome DevTools on any page with a WayKnow extension installed. The Network tab shows nothing from the extension. (The license server is the one exception — it's called once every 7 days to verify a license key, and it receives only the key, not any user data.)
  3. No accounts, no sign-up. Install and go. We don't know who our users are, and we don't want to.
  4. Open source, MIT license. Every line of code is public. Anyone can audit it, fork it, or verify that the privacy claims are real.

This is not a moral stance. It's a business decision. We make money when people trust our tools enough to pay for the Pro version or buy the next product. Trust is harder to earn when you're running a server that could, in theory, collect data. So we eliminate the server entirely, and with it, the temptation.

The harder path

Local-first is easy to claim and hard to do. Here are the things you give up:

You can't see how people use your product

No analytics means no data on which features people use, where they get stuck, or why they uninstall. Every product decision is based on intuition and direct user feedback — which is sparse when you don't collect emails. When I added Clean & Capture to SnapMark, I had no data telling me users wanted it. I built it because I wanted it myself, and hoped others would too.

You can't send re-engagement emails

No user accounts means no email list. No "we haven't seen you in a while" drip campaign. No product update announcements. If someone installs SnapMark, uses it twice, and forgets about it, I have no way to reach them. The only touchpoint is the Chrome Web Store listing and the occasional rating prompt built into the extension itself.

You can't do usage-based pricing

Subscription and metered billing require knowing who used what and when. One-time pricing means you get paid once per user, forever. This constrains what you can build — every feature has to be worth the one-time price on its own, because there's no recurring revenue to fund ongoing development.

Your acquisition is purely organic

Most SaaS companies spend 30-50% of revenue on customer acquisition — ads, content marketing teams, sales. A one-time-purchase product with no analytics can't optimize a funnel or calculate CAC payback periods. Growth relies entirely on word of mouth, search traffic, and the Chrome Web Store's organic discovery.

What you gain

The trade-offs are real, but so are the advantages:

Zero maintenance overhead

I built four products this month. Four. Each one took 1-4 weeks of development. The total ongoing maintenance burden across all four is essentially zero — no servers to patch, no databases to back up, no uptime monitors to configure. The license server (Cloudflare Workers + D1) handles verification for all products and costs $0/month. If I stop working tomorrow, all four products keep working indefinitely.

Trust as a moat

When a competitor copies your features, you're in a race to the bottom. When a competitor claims to respect privacy but runs servers you can't inspect, your position is defensible. "No servers" is binary — you either have them or you don't. It's verifiable in 30 seconds with Chrome DevTools. That's a harder moat to cross than "we have more features."

Price anchoring that works

Competing screenshot tools charge $72/year. Competing cookie managers charge $5/month. When your cost structure is zero, you can price at $39 once or $0 forever, and the comparison makes the competitor look absurd. "Why am I paying a subscription for a tool that runs entirely in my browser?" is a question the user asks themselves — you don't have to say it.

You can be small and profitable

A SaaS with $10K MRR and 30% margins is a lifestyle business. A one-time-purchase product with $50K in annual sales and near-zero costs is a very good lifestyle business. You don't need millions of users. You need thousands who trust you enough to pay once.

The real reason

I didn't start WayKnow to build a startup. I started it to build tools I use every day, and to see if you can make a living doing that without selling user data or trapping people in subscriptions.

Local-first isn't a marketing position. It's the entire strategy. If we ever added a server, the economics would shift — suddenly we'd need recurring revenue to cover hosting, which would mean subscription pricing, which would mean user accounts, which would mean data collection, which would mean privacy policies with fine print. The slope is slippery, and the only way to avoid sliding down it is to never step onto it.

So we don't.
