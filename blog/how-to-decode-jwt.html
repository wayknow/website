How to Decode a JWT in 2026 — 5 Methods, No Token Upload

Published August 12, 2026 · 7 min read

Every developer eventually needs to decode a JWT — usually at 11pm when a login fails, an integration breaks, or someone asks "what's actually in this token?" Most tutorials tell you to paste it into a website. That works, but it's the only option most people ever learn. Here are five, including the ones that never upload your token anywhere.

Quick spoiler for the impatient: if you decode JWTs more than once a month, get a zero-upload browser tool (method 4). If it's a one-off, jwt.io is fine. Everything in between is for the curious and the security-minded.

What's inside a JWT

A JWT is just three base64url-encoded parts joined by dots:

eyJhbGciOiJIUzI1NiIsInR5cCI6IkpXVCJ9.eyJzdWIiOiIxMjM0NTY3ODkwIiwibmFtZSI6IkpvaG4gRG9lIiwiaWF0IjoxNTE2MjM5MDIyfQ.SflKxwRJSMeKKF2QT4fwpMeJf36POk6yJV_adQssw5c

PartWhat it isExample
HeaderAlgorithm and token type{"alg":"HS256","typ":"JWT"}
PayloadClaims — the actual data{"sub":"1234567890","name":"John Doe","iat":1516239022}
SignatureHMAC or RSA signature of the first two partsprevents tampering

Here's the thing most tutorials skip: the first two parts are just base64. Anyone — you, me, your logs — can read them. The signature exists to stop someone from modifying the payload, not to keep it secret.

Method 1: Decode it by hand

No tools, works on any machine. JWT uses base64url: same as base64 but with - and _ instead of + and /, and no padding. To decode part 2 (the payload):

echo 'eyJzdWIiOiIxMjM0NTY3ODkwIiwibmFtZSI6IkpvaG4gRG9lIiwiaWF0IjoxNTE2MjM5MDIyfQ' | tr '_-' '/+' | base64 -d

You'll get the JSON claims. If base64 complains about invalid characters, your token's part isn't a multiple of 4 characters — append == to the end and try again. (jq will pretty-print it: add | jq . at the end.)

When to use it: once a year, on a machine with nothing else installed, or to prove it's not magic.

Method 2: Decode it in the terminal

For daily work on the command line, one alias does the whole job:

alias jwt='cut -d. -f2 | tr "_-" "/+" | base64 -d 2>/dev/null | jq .'
# usage: echo "$TOKEN" | jwt

That reads the payload. For the header, change -f2 to -f1. This is what I use when I'm already in a terminal and the token is in an environment variable — it never touches the network, so there's nothing to leak.

When to use it: server-side debugging, CI logs, anywhere you're already at a prompt.

Method 3: Online decoders (jwt.io and friends)

jwt.io is the classic: paste the token, read the decoded claims, plus algorithm and expiry at a glance. It decodes client-side in your browser, and Auth0 maintains it — it's a legitimate, well-run tool.

The honest caveat: you're still pasting a live token into a third-party page. Client-side or not, many companies' security policies forbid it, and once it's in your clipboard history and browser tabs, "it never left my machine" is hard to prove. Fine for a throwaway token; not the habit I'd build for production tokens.

When to use it: the one-off decode, especially with a token you don't care about.

If you decode tokens regularly, keep the decoder inside your browser where the token already is. The security gotcha most tutorials skip

Every time you paste a token into an online decoder, you're sharing whatever the claims contain — email addresses, user IDs, roles, tenant names, sometimes more. And two details make this worse than it sounds:

  • Expired tokens are still readable — and often still accepted. Many backends allow a clock-skew window (up to several minutes) around exp. "It expired, so it's safe to share" is not a safe assumption.
  • Tokens end up in logs. The token you pasted into a chat, a support ticket, or a bug report has a half-life that's longer than you think.

The rule I use: if I'd be embarrassed to see the token's claims in a public issue tracker, I decode it locally.

JWT claims quick reference

ClaimMeaning
issIssuer — who created the token
subSubject — who the token is about (usually a user ID)
audAudience — which service(s) accept it
expExpiry — Unix timestamp, after which it should be rejected
nbfNot-before — Unix timestamp, before which it should be rejected
iatIssued-at — when the token was created
jtiJWT ID — a unique identifier, used for revocation lists

FAQ

Do I need the secret to decode a JWT? No. Decoding is just base64. Verifying the signature is different — that needs the shared secret (HS256) or the public key (RS256/ES256).

Is jwt.io safe? It decodes client-side and is maintained by Auth0 — reputable. The risk isn't the site, it's the habit of pasting live tokens into third-party pages at all.

Can I decode an expired JWT? Yes — decoding shows all claims including exp. It doesn't validate anything; that's the server's job.

If anyone can decode the payload, what's the signature for? Tamper-proofing: change even one character of the payload and the signature no longer matches.

The verdict

One-off decode with a disposable token: jwt.io, honestly. Everything else: keep the token where it is and decode it locally — in the terminal for the server-side work, in your browser for the daily debugging, and through your AI agent when you're already talking to one. That's the setup that never has a "I pasted the prod token into a random website" story to explain.
